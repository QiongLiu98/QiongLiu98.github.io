# Designing Professional Websites for PhD/ML Researchers

## Introduction

Personal websites allow researchers to control how they present themselves, curate their work, and provide a single interface to interact with the public. Qiong Liu’s CV reveals that she is an AI scientist with a PhD from Yale University and work in medical imaging, so her site should convey **expertise, credibility, and approachability**.  Academic websites differ from commercial sites because visitors (colleagues, prospective students, journalists or grant committees) spend time evaluating the scholar’s credibility and looking for specific information rather than making impulse purchases【683450323243202†L56-L60】.  Therefore, design choices must support **clarity and evaluation**, not flashy trends【683450323243202†L141-L160】.  

This report synthesizes guidance from university branding guides, communications experts and academic–website specialists to outline best practices for designing a professional website for a PhD‑level machine‑learning researcher.  Each principle is grounded in research or authoritative guidance and includes actionable recommendations.

## Define Purpose and Audience

### Clarify Goals

- The first step is to decide **why the website exists and who will visit it**.  The Townsend Center at UC Berkeley advises scholars to begin by defining their communication goals and audiences before making design decisions【729124511429968†L147-L152】.  Early‑career academics often require more detailed personal sites than tenured faculty because they need to share publications, data and photos【729124511429968†L154-L161】.  
- Identify the primary audiences: prospective collaborators, students, hiring/promotion committees, journalists and the general public.  Each may seek different information, so content should be organized for quick retrieval.

### Maintain Professional Focus

- Academic personal websites should be **professional, not personal blogs**.  Rice University’s Graduate & Postdoctoral Studies office reminds scholars that the site’s purpose is to showcase you as a professional; content should remain focused on research and teaching【962787714807010†L218-L223】.  
- Avoid posting hobbies, personal creative writing or personal photos.  The MPOWIR career guide warns that linking to social media or hobby pages can damage professionalism【878430325526447†L124-L135】.  Early‑career researchers should be conservative about including personal content【729124511429968†L219-L224】.

## Navigation

- **Limit the number of primary menu items**.  Berkeley guidelines suggest using no more than 5–6 primary menu options【729124511429968†L235-L241】.  SitesGo notes that most academic websites fail because their navigation menus have eight or more items; clear labels like **“Research”** and **“Publications”** are better than vague terms【683450323243202†L311-L319】.  
- **Keep navigation depth shallow**.  Visitors should find desired content within three clicks【729124511429968†L235-L237】.  Use sub‑menus or anchors for further details.  
- **Highlight the menu** with an accent color and make it consistent across pages【729124511429968†L242-L244】.

## Visual Design Principles

### Layout and Visual Hierarchy

- Choose an appropriate layout based on site type:  
  - **Single‑column layout** works best for individual researcher sites because visitors read linearly from identity to research to outputs【683450323243202†L109-L116】.  Use generous padding to create rhythm without complex structural elements.  
  - **Two‑column layout** suits research lab sites, where a main column presents narrative content and a side column lists publications and quick links【683450323243202†L81-L95】.  
- Use **visual hierarchy** to guide attention.  Important elements should be larger, positioned higher and have contrasting colors【912387031439452†L79-L100】.  A descriptive headline high on the homepage helps visitors know they are in the right place【912387031439452†L102-L114】.
- Maintain **consistency** in typography, colors and layout across pages【729124511429968†L253-L254】.
- Leave **plenty of white space**.  Berkeley guidelines advise making pages 25–50 % blank to create an uncluttered feel【729124511429968†L261-L263】.

### Typography and Readability

- Body text should be **16–18 px** on desktop (17–18 px preferred for reading‑intensive content)【683450323243202†L172-L176】.  
- Keep lines **60–75 characters** wide for readability【683450323243202†L180-L185】.  Constrain column widths accordingly.  
- Limit the number of fonts to **two** and use web‑safe, legible typefaces.  Recommended fonts for academic sites include **Source Serif Pro**, **Lora**, or **Inter**【683450323243202†L189-L199】.  Avoid decorative fonts or trendy display fonts【683450323243202†L201-L208】.  
- Establish a clear **heading hierarchy**.  SitesGo recommends H1: 36‑48 px (font‑weight 700), H2: 24‑30 px (weight 600), H3: 18‑22 px (weight 600)【683450323243202†L219-L223】.  Headings should be visually distinct to communicate structure【683450323243202†L210-L216】.

### Colour Scheme and Branding

- Use a neutral background (white or light grey) with dark text for contrast【729124511429968†L259-L260】.  
- Select **one or two accent colours** for menus, links and highlights【729124511429968†L259-L261】.  Institutional colours may be used for lab sites to signal affiliation; for personal sites, choose professional colours such as deep teal or muted navy【683450323243202†L223-L240】.  
- Avoid saturated primary colours or multiple accent colours, which signal an amateur project【683450323243202†L244-L246】.

### Imagery

- **Professional headshot:** A high‑resolution (≥800×800 px), well‑lit headshot with a neutral background is essential【683450323243202†L254-L268】.  
- **Research visuals:** Feature authentic research images—microscopy, data visualisations, network graphs or equipment—to convey the nature of the work【683450323243202†L296-L303】.  If natural visuals are not available, high‑quality infographics summarising research findings may be used【683450323243202†L296-L307】.  
- **Avoid stock images** unless necessary.  Low‑quality photographs or generic lab images can undermine credibility【683450323243202†L276-L288】.

### Balance Design and Function

- Resist the temptation to add complex animations or parallax effects.  Trendy designs often obscure information and age quickly【683450323243202†L64-L79】.  A simple, content‑first design ages gracefully【683450323243202†L76-L80】.  
- Avoid portfolio‑style homepages that require prolonged scrolling before reaching key information.  The home page should act as a **navigation entry point**, not a showcase【683450323243202†L151-L160】.

## User Experience, Accessibility and Performance

### Responsive and Mobile‑First Design

- Design with **mobile devices first**.  A mobile‑first approach forces prioritisation of essential content and ensures the site degrades gracefully to larger screens【609082918711801†L46-L63】.  
- Responsive designs should use flexible units (%, rem, vw) and media queries to adapt layouts across devices【609082918711801†L74-L78】.  
- Ensure navigation collapses into a hamburger menu on small screens; text remains ≥16 px; touch targets are at least 44 × 44 px; images resize proportionally; no element relies on hover states【683450323243202†L321-L337】.

### Performance Optimization

- Aim for page load times under **three seconds**.  Notre Dame’s web standards require performance scores ≥80 on mobile and ≥90 on desktop using tools like Google Lighthouse【238198852188419†L88-L99】.  Slow websites lead to high bounce rates【609082918711801†L90-L110】.  
- Compress images (use WebP where possible), minify CSS/JavaScript, leverage browser caching and consider content delivery networks (CDNs) to reduce latency【609082918711801†L119-L133】.  
- Avoid large JavaScript frameworks and heavy animations that slow down pages【683450323243202†L376-L382】.

### Accessibility

- Follow **Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.1**.  WCAG defines four core principles: content must be **perceivable, operable, understandable and robust**【402332722928892†L303-L334】.  
- Provide alternative text for images, ensure adequate color contrast, and enable keyboard navigation【238198852188419†L88-L104】.  
- Avoid low‑contrast text on colored backgrounds; maintain high contrast for readability【683450323243202†L244-L248】.  
- Ensure that interactive elements like forms are accessible (e.g., labelled fields, error messages).  

## Credibility and Trust

- **Highlight expertise early.** SitesGo stresses that a clear name, research identity statement, professional photo, and prominent navigation links should be visible within the first seconds of visiting the site【683450323243202†L151-L160】.  
- **Use plain language**.  Avoid jargon; provide summaries of complex research for non‑specialist visitors【903849556533759†L156-L176】.  
- **Maintain current content.** Outdated information undermines credibility.  Regularly update publications and news sections【903849556533759†L291-L294】; Rice University stresses the importance of keeping CVs and research pages up to date【962787714807010†L305-L309】.  
- **Humanise the site.** Include short bios of team members and collaborators【903849556533759†L238-L244】.  This adds trust and makes the research group more approachable.  
- **Limit personal information.** Provide relevant professional information but avoid oversharing personal details【878430325526447†L124-L135】.  Social media links should be included only if those accounts are active and professional【683450323243202†L384-L390】.

## Technical Considerations

### Domain and Hosting

- Register a custom domain using your name (e.g., `firstname-lastname.com`) or an institutional subdomain.  Berkeley explains that unique personal domains allow portability when changing institutions【729124511429968†L171-L179】 and prevent others from using your name【729124511429968†L178-L183】.  
- Universities sometimes provide `.edu` subdomains; these confer credibility but may not travel with you【729124511429968†L171-L175】.  
- Choose reputable hosting services.  Notre Dame recommends high‑performing hosts such as AWS, WP Engine or Netlify【238198852188419†L86-L139】.  Low‑quality DIY platforms (Google Sites, Wix, Weebly) may not meet institutional branding standards【238198852188419†L119-L121】.  

### Platforms and Tools

- **Templates vs. Custom**: For most individual researchers, a high‑quality template customised with your content is sufficient【683450323243202†L352-L361】.  Custom design is warranted only for labs with unique visual identities【683450323243202†L358-L361】.  
- **Website Builders**: WordPress and Webflow offer flexible designs and plugin ecosystems; Webflow provides more precise design control【683450323243202†L363-L368】.  WordPress themes can be installed on personal domains or used via institutional systems【729124511429968†L277-L284】.  Google Sites or Wix are easy and free but signal minimal investment【683450323243202†L369-L372】.  
- **Documentation and Version Control**: Host source files in a version‑controlled repository (e.g., GitHub) and integrate with static site generators (Jekyll, Hugo) to manage updates and track changes.  

### SEO and Discoverability

- Use descriptive headings and meta descriptions to improve search engine indexing【671370556691874†L92-L105】.  
- Include relevant keywords in page titles and headings (e.g., “Machine Learning for Medical Imaging”), but avoid stuffing.  Long‑tail keywords specific to your field attract more qualified traffic【671370556691874†L94-L101】.  
- Ensure your Google Scholar and ORCID profiles link back to your site to improve discoverability and academic search ranking.

## Security and Legal Compliance

- Ensure your site uses **HTTPS** to protect user privacy.  
- Include privacy notices or cookie banners if you collect user data (e.g., through contact forms).  
- If dealing with medical or human subject data, ensure compliance with regulations like **HIPAA** and data anonymisation requirements【671370556691874†L83-L90】.  
- Respect copyright agreements when posting publications; some journals allow preprints on personal sites but not on institutional sites【235020244902460†L133-L137】.
